# Read-level QC metrics as predictors of alignment difficulty, and pre-screening / routing / early-exit in read mapping

Scope note: Illumina short reads. Nanopore work (GenPIP) appears only as analogous prior art. CERTA context from the assignment: about 2.4% of HG002 NovaSeq X single-end reads stay uncertified after all passes, and they cluster among reads with N bases, adapters, short length, low complexity or high error.

## Q1. What FastQC (and fastp, Falco, seqkit, MultiQC) measure, and how those metrics relate to mapping rate, mismatches, MAPQ or mapping time

### Takeaway
FastQC modules are per-sample diagnostics aggregated by position, and several of them are computed on a sample, not on every read. None is designed as a per-read predictor of mapping outcome. The quantitative evidence linking QC metrics to mapping comes almost entirely from trimming studies. On old, low-quality reads (33 bp, about 2009), quality trimming raised the human RNA-seq mapping rate from 72% to about 97%. On modern data with soft-clipping aligners, trimming has negligible effect on WGS germline variant calling and gene-level quantification. Aggressive trimming can create short reads that map spuriously. The per-read quantities that tools actually filter on (N count, fraction of low-quality bases, length, complexity) are defined in fastp and in Bowtie2, not in FastQC.

### Cited Findings
**FastQC module definitions and thresholds (Babraham documentation)**
- Per-base sequence quality: a box plot per position. It warns if any position's lower quartile is <10 or median <25, and fails if the lower quartile is <5 or median <20. For general degradation the docs advise quality trimming, often together with adapter trimming. For a transient early dip (e.g., flowcell bubbles) they advise against trimming and suggest masking during mapping instead — [FastQC: Per Base Sequence Quality](https://www.bioinformatics.babraham.ac.uk/projects/fastqc/Help/3%20Analysis%20Modules/2%20Per%20Base%20Sequence%20Quality.html)
- Per-sequence quality scores: a histogram of per-read mean quality. It warns if the most frequent mean is <27 (about 0.2% error) and fails if it is <20 (about 1% error). The docs attribute low-quality subsets to poor imaging (e.g., edge of the field of view) or to part of a flowcell — [FastQC: Per Sequence Quality Scores](https://www.bioinformatics.babraham.ac.uk/projects/fastqc/Help/3%20Analysis%20Modules/3%20Per%20Sequence%20Quality%20Scores.html)
- Per-base N content: % N calls per position. It warns if any position exceeds 5% and fails above 20%. The usual cause is general quality loss. N spikes at a few early positions can also come from biased library composition that confuses the base caller — [FastQC: Per Base N Content](https://www.bioinformatics.babraham.ac.uk/projects/fastqc/Help/3%20Analysis%20Modules/6%20Per%20Base%20N%20Content.html)
- Per-sequence GC: the observed per-read GC distribution against a normal distribution built from the observed mode. It warns if deviations exceed 15% of reads and fails above 30%. Sharp peaks suggest a specific contaminant (e.g., adapter dimers), and broad peaks suggest another species. A shifted distribution is not flagged — [FastQC: Per Sequence GC Content](https://www.bioinformatics.babraham.ac.uk/projects/fastqc/Help/3%20Analysis%20Modules/5%20Per%20Sequence%20GC%20Content.html)
- Duplication levels: only sequences first seen in the first 100,000 reads are tracked to the end of the file. Reads longer than 75 bp are truncated to 50 bp, and an exact full-length match is required. It warns if non-unique sequences are >20% of the total and fails above 50%. It cannot separate PCR duplicates from biological collisions — [FastQC: Duplicate Sequences](https://www.bioinformatics.babraham.ac.uk/projects/fastqc/Help/3%20Analysis%20Modules/8%20Duplicate%20Sequences.html)
- Overrepresented sequences: uses the same sampling (first 100,000 reads) and the same 75→50 bp truncation. It warns above 0.1% of the library and fails above 1%. Each hit is matched against a contaminant database (≥20 bp, ≤1 mismatch) — [FastQC: Overrepresented Sequences](https://www.bioinformatics.babraham.ac.uk/projects/fastqc/Help/3%20Analysis%20Modules/9%20Overrepresented%20Sequences.html)
- Adapter content: the cumulative % of reads containing each configured adapter, by position. A sequence counts as present from its first occurrence to the end of the read. It warns above 5% of reads and fails above 10%. The stated cause is that "a reasonable proportion of the insert sizes are shorter than the read length". The docs say the warning "doesn't indicate a problem as such" and that the reads need adapter trimming — [FastQC: Adapter Content](https://www.bioinformatics.babraham.ac.uk/projects/fastqc/Help/3%20Analysis%20Modules/10%20Adapter%20Content.html)

**Falco, seqkit and MultiQC**
- Falco (Brandine & Smith, F1000Research, v2 2021) is a C++ emulation of FastQC. It runs on average about 3× faster with equivalent results and less memory — [Falco, PMC7845152](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC7845152/). A third party (Oregon State CQLS) notes it is "not completely a 1:1 replacement" — [CQLS falco 0.3.0](https://software.cqls.oregonstate.edu/updates/falco-0.3.0)
- `seqkit stats --all` reports per-file num_seqs, length stats, N50, Q20(%), Q30(%), AvgQual and GC(%). MultiQC has a module that aggregates it, using `--tabular` output — [MultiQC seqkit module docs](https://docs.seqera.io/multiqc/modules/seqkit)

**fastp per-read filters (README defaults; Chen et al. 2018)**
- Quality filtering is on by default. A base counts as "qualified" at ≥Q15 (`-q 15`). A read is dropped if >40% of its bases are unqualified (`-u 40`), or if it has >5 N bases (`-n 5`). The average-quality filter is off by default (`-e 0`). The length filter drops reads <15 bp (`-l 15`) — [fastp README](https://github.com/OpenGene/fastp); [fastp man page 0.20.1](https://manpages.ubuntu.com/manpages/jammy/man1/fastp.1.html)
- The low-complexity filter is off by default (`-y`). Complexity is the % of positions whose base differs from the next base, and the default threshold is 30% (`-Y 30`) — [fastp README](https://github.com/OpenGene/fastp)
- PolyG tail trimming is on by default for NovaSeq/NextSeq, detected from the machine ID, because "G means no signal in the Illumina two-color systems". The minimum polyG length is 10 — [fastp README](https://github.com/OpenGene/fastp)
- For paired-end reads, adapters are found by per-read overlap analysis, and k-mer auto-detection is off by default. For single-end reads, adapters are auto-detected — [fastp README](https://github.com/OpenGene/fastp)
- Sliding-window quality cutting (`-5`/`-3`/`-r`) uses a window of 4 and a mean of Q20 by default. Overrepresentation analysis samples 1 in 20 reads (`-P 20`). Duplication evaluation is hash-based, misclassifies about 0.01% of reads through collisions, and costs about 1 GB and 10–20% more time — [fastp README](https://github.com/OpenGene/fastp); [fastp bioRxiv 2018](https://www.biorxiv.org/content/biorxiv/early/2018/04/09/274100.full.pdf)

**Trimming studies (quantitative relation to mapping)**
- Del Fabbro et al. 2013 (PLoS ONE 8:e85024) compared 9 trimmers on 4 datasets. Human HepG2 RNA-seq (SRR002073, 33 bp, TopHat2→hg19): 72.189% of untrimmed reads mapped. Trimmed reads mapped at 87.7–96.97% depending on tool (ConDeTri 96.973%, SolexaQA 96.743%, Trimmomatic Q22 95.875%, Sickle Q20 95.971%, Cutadapt Q26 91.751%, FASTX Q40 87.733%). Arabidopsis (83 bp): 82.774% untrimmed versus about 98.7–99.4% trimmed. Reads shorter than 70% of their original length were discarded. The best effects came at "intermediate quality thresholds (Q between 20 and 30)", and "a Q set too high can dramatically reduce the surviving dataset size". The authors also say TopHat "seem[s] to be able to overcome low quality issues". Trimming reduced execution time and RAM (their Fig. 6), but the text gives no numbers — [Del Fabbro et al. 2013 (Europe PMC full text)](https://www.ebi.ac.uk/europepmc/webservices/rest/PMC3871669/fullTextXML)
- Del Fabbro 2013, SNP calling (BWA 0.6.2 + VarScan, 100 bp PE peach and yeast): the authors' false-positive indicator (APOMAC) fell from 0.2367% to about 0.04–0.065% (yeast) and from 0.2909% to about 0.065–0.16% (peach) after trimming. The text and the table use inconsistent scales — [Del Fabbro et al. 2013](https://www.ebi.ac.uk/europepmc/webservices/rest/PMC3871669/fullTextXML)
- Williams et al. 2016 (BMC Bioinformatics 17:103; Drosophila RNA-seq; SolexaQA, Trimmomatic, ConDeTri; TopHat2/Cuffdiff2): with the most aggressive trimming, >10% of genes changed estimated expression significantly. A minimum-length filter after trimming removed most of the change, "suggesting that the differential gene expression was primarily being driven by spurious mapping of short reads" — [Williams et al. 2016](https://bmcbioinformatics.biomedcentral.com/track/pdf/10.1186/s12859-016-0956-2)
- Liao & Shi 2020 (NAR Genom Bioinform 2:lqaa068; SEQC benchmark plus simulation; Subread): "adapter sequences can be effectively removed by read aligner via 'soft-clipping'", and many low-quality bases were "rescued by the aligner". Untrimmed accuracy was comparable or slightly better, and skipping trimming cut total analysis time by up to an order of magnitude — [Liao & Shi 2020, PMC7671312](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC7671312); [bioRxiv 833962](https://www.biorxiv.org/content/10.1101/833962.full.pdf)

### Inferences
- FastQC's sampling (first 100k reads, 75→50 bp truncation) makes duplication and overrepresentation unsuitable as per-read features. The per-read analogues that are cheap and complete are N count, the count of low-quality bases, length, a complexity score, an adapter k-mer hit at the 3' end, and GC.
- The big mapping-rate gains from trimming in Del Fabbro 2013 came from GA-era 33 bp reads with poor tail quality. They should not be extrapolated to NovaSeq X, where most bases are in the Q40 bin (Q5).
- For an exact or certified aligner, "trimming improves mapping" has a precise meaning. Removing low-quality or adapter bases lowers the edit distance that a full-length (end-to-end) certificate must cover, so it moves reads from the expensive passes into the cheap ones. The Williams 2016 result is a warning: shortened reads become more multi-mapping and ambiguous, which pushes them toward the repeat tier.

### Gaps
- I found no study that correlates FastQC per-sample metrics with mapping *time* or MAPQ distributions across many runs. The available evidence links them only to mapping rate and downstream accuracy.
- I did not retrieve the MultiQC paper (Ewels et al. 2016) or the Falco speed tables. Only the abstract-level "3× faster" figure is cited.
- Del Fabbro's runtime and memory reductions appear only in a figure, with no numbers in the text.

## Q2. Low-complexity and tandem-repeat reads: effect on mapping time and multi-mapping, and whether aligners mask or special-case them

### Takeaway
I found no quantitative source on how much low-complexity reads cost in mapping time. The evidence is indirect. Aligners cap work on highly repetitive seeds through occurrence caps and re-seeding rules. Low-complexity regions are a leading documented source of alignment and variant artifacts. Expert advice is to mask low-complexity regions *after* mapping, not to mask reads or the reference before mapping. Read-level complexity filters exist in fastp, PRINSEQ and BBDuk, but they are off by default or optional.

### Cited Findings
- Li 2014 (Bioinformatics 30:2843) built 10 SNP/INDEL call sets with 2 mappers and 5 callers. Analysing false heterozygous calls on a haploid genome, it identified "erroneous realignment in low-complexity regions and the incomplete reference genome" as the two major error sources. Raw genotype calls had about 1 error per 10–15 kb, and filtering brought this to about 1 per 100–200 kb without significant loss of sensitivity — [Li 2014, arXiv 1404.0929](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1404.0929); [PMC4271055](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/4271055)
- A community recommendation attributed to Heng Li: "mask the reference genome after mapping and filter out variants/features [that] fall in low-complexity regions. It is not advised to mask the read sequences, or mask the reference genome before mapping." This is a Biostars comment, not peer-reviewed — [Biostars 303375](https://www.biostars.org/p/303375)
- BWA-MEM `-c`: "Discard a MEM if it has more than INT occurence in the genome". The manual calls this "an insensitive parameter" and gives the default as 10000. `-r 1.5` triggers re-seeding for MEMs longer than minSeedLen×1.5 and is "a key heuristic parameter for tuning the performance" — [BWA manual](https://bio-bwa.sourceforge.net/bwa.shtml)
- Bowtie2 `-R` (default 2) re-seeds reads with repetitive seeds. A read counts as repetitive when total seed hits divided by the number of seeds that aligned at least once exceeds 300. The manual warns that `-k`/`-a` "can be very, very slow" on long repetitive genomes — [Bowtie2 manual](https://bowtie-bio.sourceforge.net/bowtie2/manual.shtml)
- The DUST score measures trinucleotide over-representation, with homopolymers and short tandem repeats scoring highest. Practitioners use DUST, entropy or compression ratio to filter reads. One user reports that low-complexity reads produce false-positive splice junctions in RNA-seq — [Biostars 8899](https://biostars.org/p/8899); [PRINSEQ complexity filter (Chipster)](https://chipster.csc.fi/manual/prinseq-complexity-filter.html)
- fastp's complexity metric, the fraction of adjacent base pairs that differ, defaults to a 30% threshold when enabled — [fastp README](https://github.com/OpenGene/fastp)
- GATK PathSeqFilterSpark removes low-complexity reads together with low-quality, duplicate and host reads before pathogen alignment, an example of pipeline-level pre-screening — [GATK PathSeqFilterSpark](https://gatk.broadinstitute.org/hc/en-us/articles/360036482152-PathSeqFilterSpark)
- A PeerJ preprint predicted read alignment quality from intrinsic read features: run-length distribution, DUST, entropy and base quality. Run-length mean and variance did as well as or better than DUST and entropy, with F1 0.5–0.95 on Ion Torrent and Illumina data. I could not open the full text (HTTP 403), so this comes from the search snippet only — [PeerJ Preprints 27428](https://peerj.com/preprints/27428)

### Inferences
- For CERTA, a low-complexity read is hard for two separate reasons. (1) Seed occurrence counts explode, so the hit budget is exhausted. (2) The best alignment may be ambiguous among many near-equal loci, so certifying uniqueness or optimality is costly. A cheap per-read predictor is a complexity score (fastp-style, or DUST over 3-mers) combined with the max or median occurrence count of the read's seeds, which the index lookup returns almost for free. Bowtie2's "seed-hit ratio > 300" rule is a published precedent for routing on a seed-occurrence feature.
- The run-length features in the PeerJ preprint (homopolymer runs) are cheap and may suit NovaSeq polyG tails especially well.

### Gaps
- I found no benchmark measuring time per read as a function of a DUST or entropy score in BWA-MEM, Bowtie2 or minimap2.
- The BWA `-c` default: the online manual says 10000. I did not verify whether current source releases use a different value (I recall 500, but this is unconfirmed).
- The PRINSEQ and BBDuk default DUST and entropy thresholds were not verified from their primary docs.

## Q3. Published pre-screening, routing and early-exit methods in read mapping: speedups and accuracy costs

### Takeaway
There are three families of prior art.
- **Candidate-location filters** (SHD, GateKeeper, Shouji, MAGNET, SneakySnake, GRIM-Filter, GenCache) reject (read, location) pairs that provably or heuristically exceed an edit threshold before full DP. They report alignment-step speedups of 10× to more than 400×, but end-to-end mapper speedups are much smaller (1.4× to about 5×).
- **Read-level filters** (GenStore-EM/NM) take reads out of the expensive path entirely. Exact-match reads go to a cheap path, and reads with few seeds and low chaining scores are dropped. Gains scale with the prevalence of the filtered class: 2–6× at 75–85% exact-match reads, and 22–29× when 99.65% of reads do not align.
- **Early rejection** of low-quality or unmappable reads (GenPIP, nanopore) and aligner-internal give-up rules (Bowtie2 `-D`, `--n-ceil`).

Only exact-match filtering, and filters with 0% false rejects, preserve output exactly. Chaining-score and quality-threshold rejection are lossy heuristics.

### Cited Findings
**Candidate-location (pre-alignment) filters, SAFARI group and others**
- GateKeeper (Alser et al., Bioinformatics 2017, FPGA): accuracy on average >96%. It is on average 90× and 130× faster than the software pre-alignment filters Adjacency Filter and Shifted Hamming Distance (SHD). Added in front of mrFAST, it cuts verification time by up to 10× — [GateKeeper arXiv 1604.01789](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1604.01789)
- GateKeeper-GPU: 2.9× acceleration of sequence alignment, and up to 1.4× end-to-end speedup of mrFAST — [GateKeeper-GPU arXiv 2103.14978](https://web3.arxiv.org/pdf/2103.14978)
- Shouji (Alser et al., Bioinformatics 2019, 35:4255): pre-alignment filtering accuracy up to 2 orders of magnitude better than GateKeeper and SHD. The FPGA version is up to 3 orders of magnitude faster than Shouji on CPU. Used as a pre-alignment step, it cut the execution time of 5 aligners by up to 18.8× — [Shouji, Bioinformatics](https://academic.oup.com/bioinformatics/article/35/21/4255/5421509); [PMC6821304](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6821304)
- SneakySnake (Alser et al., Bioinformatics 2020; CPU, GPU and FPGA): speeds up Edlib and Parasail by up to 37.6× and 43.9× (>12× on average) without hardware acceleration, and by up to 413× and 689× (>400× on average) with it. It claims up to 4 orders of magnitude better filtering accuracy than Shouji, GateKeeper and SHD. The paper treats a 0% false reject rate as a requirement and minimises false accepts against Edlib ground truth. Datasets were 100 bp, 250 bp, 10 kbp and 100 kbp — [SneakySnake arXiv 1910.09020](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1910.09020)
- In Alser's PhD thesis, SneakySnake with the default maze size has a false accept rate of up to 30% on low-edit sequences but under 4% on edit-rich sequences — [Alser dissertation arXiv 1910.03936](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1910.03936)
- MAGNET (Alser, Mutlu, Alkan, arXiv 2017), "Understanding and Improving the Accuracy of Genome Pre-alignment Filtering", exists, but I did not retrieve its numbers. It is cited in GenStore's references — [GenStore arXiv 2202.10400](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2202.10400)
- GRIM-Filter (seed-location filtering in 3D-stacked DRAM): 1.81–3.65× end-to-end read mapper speedup over a mapper using the best previous seed-location filter — [GRIM-Filter arXiv 1711.01177](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1711.01177)
- GenCache (Nag et al., MICRO 2019; compute-in-SRAM with modified seeding/filtering): 5.26× (about 5.3×) execution-time reduction and 8.6× energy efficiency over the same accelerator without in-cache operations, with outputs unchanged. Neither the algorithm changes nor the in-cache operators give much speedup alone — [GenCache (NSF PAR)](https://par.nsf.gov//servlets/purl/10121197); [arXiv 2401.11685 summary](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2401.11685)

**Read-level filtering: GenStore (Mansouri Ghiasi et al., ASPLOS 2022)**
- GenStore-EM filters reads that exactly match a reference subsequence, motivated by the claim that "on average 80% of human short reads map exactly to the human reference genome" (a claim from prior work [43, 55, 99]). It uses a sorted, read-sized k-mer index that allows sequential scans with one index lookup per read. Not applicable to long reads: a 10 kb human read matches exactly with probability <3.6×10⁻⁶ — [GenStore arXiv 2202.10400](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2202.10400)
- On accuracy, the paper says exact-match detection "does not affect the accuracy of read mapping due to the extremely low collision rate of strong hash functions" — [GenStore](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2202.10400)
- EM was evaluated on Mason 2–simulated 22 GB short-read sets with 75% and 85% exact-match rates (human, mutations from the NA12878 list), against Minimap2 as the software baseline. Speedup grows with input size (2.62× → 4.75×) and with the exact-match rate (3.46× → 6.05× for the largest set). With the GenCache hardware mapper, the speedup is 1.52× → 3.13× — [GenStore](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2202.10400)
- In an idealised motivation study, an in-storage filter beats Minimap2 by 3.12× and beats a software exact-match filter (SW-filter) by 2.21× — [GenStore](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2202.10400)
- GenStore-NM is a routing rule built on a cheap feature. It runs a cheap chaining step only on reads with *few* exactly matching seeds and sends reads with many seeds to the host. The rationale: "a read with a large number of seeds most likely aligns to the reference genome and does not require in-storage filtering". The chaining score follows Minimap2's chaining — [GenStore](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2202.10400)
- NM motivation: across use cases (SARS-CoV-2, no reference, contamination, long reads), 31.7–99.6% of reads do not align. For a 12.4 GB read set with 99.65% non-aligning reads, GenStore beats Minimap2 by 22.4×, 29.0× and 27.9× on low-, mid- and high-end SSDs, with about 27× energy savings. Headline ranges: 2.07–6.05× (software baselines) for high-similarity read sets and 1.45–33.63× for low-similarity ones — [GenStore](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2202.10400); [arXiv abstract](https://arxiv.org/abs/2202.10400v2)

**Early rejection (nanopore analogue): GenPIP (Mao et al., MICRO 2022)**
- GenPIP integrates basecalling and read mapping in memory. Its early-rejection mechanism "stops the execution of genome analysis for low-quality and unmapped reads". It checks the average quality of a few chunks basecalled so far, and drops a failing read without basecalling its remaining chunks. The sampled chunks should be non-consecutive to estimate read quality accurately — [GenPIP arXiv 2209.08600](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2209.08600); [ETH Research Collection](https://research-collection.ethz.ch/handle/20.500.11850/586135)
- Whole-system results: 41.6× (8.4×) speedup and 32.8× (20.8×) energy savings over CPU (GPU) software, "with negligible accuracy loss". Against a combination of state-of-the-art in-memory basecalling and mapping accelerators: 1.39× speedup and 1.37× energy savings. The contribution of early rejection alone was not isolated in the sources I retrieved — [GenPIP](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2209.08600)

**Aligner-internal early exits and per-read pre-screens**
- Bowtie2 `-D` (default 15): "Up to <int> consecutive seed extension attempts can 'fail' before Bowtie 2 moves on". An extension fails if it yields no new best or second-best alignment. Presets set -D 5, 10, 15 or 20 and -R 1, 2, 2 or 3, from `--very-fast` to `--very-sensitive` — [Bowtie2 manual](https://bowtie-bio.sourceforge.net/bowtie2/manual.shtml)
- Bowtie2 `--n-ceil` (default `L,0,0.15`, i.e. 0.15 × read length) is a per-read pre-screen: reads with more ambiguous characters are filtered out and tagged `YF:Z:NS` — [Bowtie2 manual](https://bowtie-bio.sourceforge.net/bowtie2/manual.shtml)
- BWA-MEM `-T 30` affects output only (alignments scoring <30 are not reported). `-c` discards over-repetitive MEMs — [BWA manual](https://bio-bwa.sourceforge.net/bwa.shtml)

**ML / statistical prediction of mapping outcome (post-alignment features dominate)**
- LoQuM (Bioinformatics 2012) recalibrates MAPQ for any aligner with a classifier. Its features are read base qualities plus alignment features (matches, mismatches, deletions, aligner MAPQ, number of mappings), trained on simulated reads — [LoQuM, PMC3436835](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC3436835/)
- PeerJ 2020 (article 10501): ML on SAM features to flag misaligned reads and recompute MAPQ. Misaligned reads were 0.16% of the training set, F1 was about 0.76–0.82, and SNP calling improved modestly at low coverage — [PeerJ 10501](https://peerj.com/articles/10501); [PMC7727374](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC7727374/)
- Genome Biology 2017 tandem-simulation framework (Qtip): aligner MAPQ methods "are generally quite ad hoc". It predicts MAPQ by simulating reads that resemble the input and training a model on alignment features — [Genome Biology 2017](https://genomebiology.biomedcentral.com/track/pdf/10.1186/s13059-017-1290-3)
- The intrinsic-feature predictor (run length, DUST, entropy, base quality; F1 0.5–0.95) is the PeerJ preprint above — [PeerJ Preprints 27428](https://peerj.com/preprints/27428)

### Inferences
- **What carries over to a certified aligner.** Of the published approaches, only output-preserving ones fit CERTA:
  - GenStore-EM-style exact-match fast paths.
  - Candidate filters with a proven 0% false-reject rate (SneakySnake states this as a requirement).
  - GenCache, which reports unchanged outputs.

  GenStore-NM (chaining-score threshold), GenPIP (quality threshold) and Bowtie2 `-D`/`--n-ceil` are lossy heuristics. In CERTA they could only reorder work, not drop reads. Example: send reads predicted to be uncertifiable straight to the pass most likely to resolve them, skipping intermediate passes that will fail. The final output does not change, only the wasted cost of failed passes.
- **A certified early exit is possible where a cheap lower bound exists.** If N counts as a mismatch, a read with more N bases than the edit budget E provably has no end-to-end alignment within E. This is a lossless version of Bowtie2's `--n-ceil`. Pigeonhole or q-gram lemma bounds over the seed lookups CERTA already does can give similar certificates of "no alignment within E".
- **Expected payoff scales with prevalence and per-read cost.** GenStore's results make this explicit: 2–6× speedups at 75–85% filterable reads, and 22–29× at 99.65%. With about 2.4% uncertified reads, the best routing saving is bounded by the share of total time those reads spend in passes that ultimately fail. This should be measured (time per pass per read class) before investing in a predictor.
- **Cheap routing features already used in the literature:**
  - number of exact seed hits, or seeds per read (GenStore-NM);
  - seed-hit ratio, with a threshold of 300 (Bowtie2 `-R`);
  - mean quality of sampled chunks (GenPIP);
  - N count (Bowtie2, fastp);
  - complexity and run length (fastp, PeerJ preprint).

### Gaps
- I did not retrieve SeGraM (ISCA 2022) speedup and accuracy figures, or MAGNET's numbers.
- Whether SHD, GateKeeper and Shouji guarantee 0% false rejects was not verified from primary text. The SneakySnake paper frames 0% false reject as a requirement, but the per-filter false-reject results were not extracted.
- I found no published short-read method that predicts, from read features alone, whether a read will map (or be certifiable) and routes on that prediction. The closest are GenStore-NM's seed-count gate and the PeerJ preprint's complexity-feature classifier.
- GenPIP's early-rejection-only speedup and its rejection false-positive rate were not found in the abstract-level sources.

## Q4. Quality-aware or adapter-aware alignment (soft-clipping, local mode) versus pre-trimming: what changes

### Takeaway
On modern WGS, letting the aligner soft-clip or down-weight bad bases gives essentially the same germline variant accuracy as pre-trimming adapters. Trimming matters more in exome data (8–35% of reads carry adapters) and for some ML callers. Quality-aware scoring (Bowtie2's Q-scaled mismatch penalty) and local mode are the in-aligner alternatives to trimming. Pre-trimming changes read length, which can create spurious short-read mappings. An exact end-to-end aligner does not soft-clip, so it bears the full cost of adapter and low-quality tails unless they are trimmed or a local certificate is used.

### Cited Findings
- Barbitoff & Predeus 2024 (F1000Research 13:506) used 7 GIAB samples (HG001–HG007), WGS (HiSeq) and WES, BWA-MEM 0.7.17 → GRCh37, and fastp 0.23.2, Trimmomatic 0.39 and BBDuk 37.62 in palindromic mode. In WGS with DeepVariant there was no raw-versus-trimmed difference in SNP or indel precision or recall. In WES, 8.1–35.2% of reads contained adapters, and high-coverage WES gained only "2–4 additional true positive variants in only two out of seven datasets". Clair3 and Octopus (RF filtering) showed significant F1 gains with trimming, and "some of the recently developed machine learning-based variant callers demonstrate greater dependence on the presence of adapters in reads" — [Barbitoff & Predeus 2024](https://f1000research.com/articles/13-506); [bioRxiv 2023.04.28.538608](https://www.biorxiv.org/content/10.1101/2023.04.28.538608.full.pdf)
- Liao & Shi 2020: aligner soft-clipping removes adapters and rescues many low-quality bases. Untrimmed gene-level quantification is as accurate or slightly better, and up to about 10× faster overall — [PMC7671312](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC7671312)
- A counter-recommendation from a core facility (UC Davis): for variant analysis, annotation or assembly, "we recommend read trimming, including both adapter and quality trimming" — [UC Davis DNA Tech Core](https://dnatech.genomecenter.ucdavis.edu/?p=4900)
- Bowtie2 quality-aware mismatch penalty: MN + floor((MX−MN)·min(Q,40)/40), with MX=6 and MN=2. A mismatch at a low-quality base therefore costs as little as 2, and one at Q≥40 costs 6. N positions cost `--np` 1. `--ignore-quals` treats every base as maximum quality. In `--local` mode, read ends can be soft-clipped "to achieve the greatest possible alignment score". Minimum score: end-to-end `L,-0.6,-0.6` and local `G,20,8` (20 + 8·ln L) — [Bowtie2 manual](https://bowtie-bio.sourceforge.net/bowtie2/manual.shtml)
- BWA-MEM clipping penalty `-L 5`: if the best score reaching the end of the query is larger than the best SW score minus the clipping penalty, clipping will not be applied — [BWA manual](https://bio-bwa.sourceforge.net/bwa.shtml)
- FastQC advises that for a transient quality dip, "masking bases during mapping or assembly" is preferable to trimming — [FastQC Per Base Sequence Quality](https://www.bioinformatics.babraham.ac.uk/projects/fastqc/Help/3%20Analysis%20Modules/2%20Per%20Base%20Sequence%20Quality.html)
- Williams 2016: aggressive quality trimming without a minimum-length filter changed expression of >10% of genes through spurious mapping of short reads — [Williams et al. 2016](https://bmcbioinformatics.biomedcentral.com/track/pdf/10.1186/s12859-016-0956-2)

### Inferences
- CERTA's local-alignment certificates play the role of soft-clipping. Reads carrying an adapter tail fail end-to-end certification and need the local pass. Detecting a 3' adapter cheaply would let such reads go straight to the local-certificate path, or to an adapter-clipped end-to-end certificate on the read prefix. For example, CERTA could match the first 8–12 bases of the known Illumina adapter (AGATCGGAAGAGC) and certify the clipped prefix exactly.
- If CERTA's scoring ignores quality values, then a quality-aware (Bowtie2-style) penalty, or simply treating Q12-bin bases as wildcards, would put high-error reads into a different certification class. That changes the semantics of the certificate, so it is a design decision, not a pure speed-up.

### Gaps
- I found no head-to-head WGS benchmark in which pre-trimming versus soft-clipping is the only variable in mapping *time* or MAPQ.
- I found no published evaluation of exact or certified aligners (rather than heuristic ones) on adapter-containing reads.

## Q5. Prevalence in modern Illumina WGS (NovaSeq 6000/X, PCR-free): reads with N, adapters, low mean quality, low complexity

### Takeaway
Vendor QC on NovaSeq X Plus WGS (NA12878) reports almost no read pairs with >10% N (0.00–0.01%) or with >50% low-quality bases (0.00%). About 0.12–0.17% of pairs are flagged adapter-related, about 94.4–95.0% of bases are ≥Q30, and 99.7% of reads map. Illumina reports that ≥85% of NovaSeq X bases fall in the Q40 bin (more than 90% for an HG002 PCR-free 10B run). By these thresholds, the "bad read" classes are an order of magnitude rarer than CERTA's 2.4% uncertified fraction. Important caveat: Illumina's BCL Convert trims adapters by default, N-pads reads trimmed below 35 bp, and fully N-masks reads shorter than 22 bp. So in BCL-Convert or DRAGEN FASTQs, "short + adapter + trailing Ns" may be a single population of short inserts, which the vendor's ">10% N" metric would under-count.

### Cited Findings
- Novogene, NA12878 WGS QC, 4 NovaSeq X Plus runs versus NovaSeq 6000. Q30 94.41–94.97% (6000: 93.34%). "Containing N" 0.00–0.01%, where the definition is a pair in which either read has >10% uncertain bases. "Low quality" 0.00%, where the definition is >50% low-quality bases. "Adapter related" 0.12–0.17% (6000: 0.17%). Mapped 99.71–99.75% (6000: 99.89%). Depth about 30×. SNP precision and recall 99.14–99.18% and 97.84–97.98%. Library type (PCR-free or not) and read length are not stated, and this is vendor data — [Novogene NovaSeq X Plus data at a glance](https://amea.novogene.com/about/news/reaching-maximum-465gb-lane-with-an-average-q30-95-novogenes-novaseq-x-plus-data-at-a-glance)
- For contrast, Novogene's NA12878 exome on X Plus shows 2.17–2.99% adapter-related pairs (6000: 2.11%) and containing-N 0.00–0.01% — [Novogene NovaSeq X Plus new data release](https://amea.novogene.com/about/news/novaseq-x-plus-new-data-release)
- Illumina (Oct 2023, NovaSeq X software v1.2, XLEAP-SBS): the three Q bins are Q12 (Q3–Q17), Q24 (Q18–Q29) and Q40 (≥Q30). NovaSeq X delivers ≥85% of bases in the Q40 bin. In a 10B HG002 PCR-free run, more than 90% of bases are in the Q40 bin and more than 80% are ≥Q40 at full resolution. The empirical error rate of the Q40 bin is Q40 (previously Q37). Binning has little or no impact on germline results. The article gives no PhiX, adapter or N rates — [Illumina: XLEAP-SBS Q40](https://www.illumina.com/science/genomics-research/articles/data-quality-q-scores.html)
- BCL Convert defaults (Illumina support docs):
  - AdapterBehavior=trim; the alternative, mask, replaces adapter bases with N.
  - AdapterStringency=0.9 (≥90% identity to the adapter).
  - MinimumAdapterOverlap=1.
  - MinimumTrimmedReadLength=35: "Bases below the specified value are masked with N", i.e., reads trimmed below 35 bp are N-padded to 35.
  - MaskShortReads=22: reads shorter than this "become masked", i.e., all N.

  Illumina's knowledge base notes that short-insert reads are "masked with Ns after FASTQ generation" and suggests setting both values to 5 to avoid it. One Illumina FAQ gives a conflicting default of 22 for minimum trimmed read length — [BCL Convert Sample Sheet settings](https://support-docs.illumina.com/SW/BCL_Convert/Content/SW/BCLConvert/SampleSheets_swBCL.htm); [Illumina KB 000009157](https://knowledge.illumina.com/software/bcl-convert/software-bcl-convert-reference_material-list/000009157); [Illumina FAQ 000003633](https://knowledge.illumina.com/software/general/software-general-faq-list/000003633)
- An anecdotal community report (Biostars) describes high N content at the start of Read 1 in NovaSeq X Plus WGS even after fastp, and suggests hard-trimming the first 10–15 bases. This comes from the search snippet and is not systematic — [Biostars 9607306](https://biostars.org/p/9607306)
- A 2019 HiSeq X versus NovaSeq 6000 comparison (Scientific Reports, NYGC): alignment rate always >99.5%, duplicates 11.25% (HiSeq X) versus 6.6% (NovaSeq), and mismatch rate 0.75% versus 0.6%, very similar after quality filtering — [Sci Rep 2019, s41598-019-55636-3](https://link.springer.com/10.1038/s41598-019-55636-3); [NYGC PDF](https://bioinformatics.nygenome.org/wp-content/uploads/2020/01/s41598-019-55636-3.pdf)
- PolyG tails are a two-colour-chemistry artefact on NovaSeq and NextSeq ("G means no signal"), and fastp trims them by default on those instruments — [fastp README](https://github.com/OpenGene/fastp)
- The "about 80% of human short reads map exactly" figure in GenStore is a citation-based claim. GenStore's own experiments used simulated read sets with 75–85% exact-match rates — [GenStore](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2202.10400)

### Inferences
- The vendor's "containing N" metric (>10% N) and its "adapter related" flag are much stricter than "≥1 N" or "any adapter overlap". CERTA's uncertified reads plausibly include many reads with only 1–2 Ns, short 3' adapter overlaps, or polyG tails. These would not show up in such QC tables, which explains why vendor prevalences (≤0.2%) are far below 2.4%.
- If the HG002 NovaSeq X FASTQs were produced by BCL Convert or onboard DRAGEN with default settings, then reads from inserts shorter than the read length were trimmed. Those trimmed below 35 bp carry trailing N padding, and those below 22 bp are all N. This would make "N bases", "adapter", and "short length" correlated symptoms of one cause, short inserts. A quick check: look for a spike in the read-length distribution at exactly 35, for reads ending in N runs, and for all-N reads. If confirmed, a lossless pre-screen such as "trailing N run of length ≥ (E+1)", or "all-N", could certify unalignability within the edit budget at near-zero cost, or route those reads to a prefix-only certificate.
- NovaSeq X's three bins make "number of non-Q40-bin bases" a natural cheap error proxy. Given Q40-bin empirical error ≈1e-4, a read with zero non-Q40 bases has about 1.5% expected chance of containing any sequencing error over 150 bp. So quality-bin counts should strongly predict which reads need edit-tolerant passes. This is a back-of-envelope figure, not a published one.

### Gaps
- I found no published HG002 NovaSeq X PCR-free statistics for: the fraction of reads with ≥1 N; the fraction with any adapter overlap (it depends on the insert-size distribution); the fraction failing a DUST or entropy threshold; or the fraction with polyG tails.
- Independent (non-vendor) NovaSeq X versus 6000 WGS comparisons with mismatch and duplication rates were not found.
- Whether the specific HG002 NovaSeq X data used for CERTA went through BCL Convert trimming and masking is unknown. This should be checked in the data itself.
